Introduction

What is Cerebral Palsy?

Cerebral Palsy is a neurological medical condition caused at birth when the baby is still in its mother's womb. When the baby's brain can't get enough oxygen, Cerebral Palsy (or CP for short) can occur.

Symptoms

  • Unilateral walking gait (walking from side to side)
  • Speech development issues
  • Problems with vision
  • Hand coordination issues

Treatments

  • Physical Therapy
  • Occupational Therapy
  • Botox injections
  • Stem cell therapy

Part A: Existing Technology

Before 2010

2001

Lokomat

Locomat therapy is a fun way to improve a person with cerebral palsy's walking gait. It's like a treadmill but for people with bilateral walking patterns. A huge robot is harnessed onto the person's body with arms free and then they walk in a straight line.

The treadmill can be elevated to increase difficulty. A smartboard connected to the treadmill has games including racing games, park levels, and penguin games where you can work out each individual leg. It also has an EKG-like screen to monitor walking patterns.

2000

Whole Body Vibration Machine

An individual taking physical therapy could stand up on the vibrating machine to loosen up the hamstring muscles. This is beneficial for someone with CP because individuals with CP constantly have tight hamstrings.

Getting loose before a workout or therapy makes moving easier without the difficulty of opening one's legs.

2010 - 2020

2016

Sensory Needs Apps

Apps like iComm and Match the Card help kids with special needs communication and sensory needs. Match the Card (August 2016) helps kids learn to communicate using pictures and matching them to correct words.

Commonly used in speech therapy to help kids who need help with their pronunciation skills.

2010s

Exoskeleton

A wearable robot for kids with cerebral palsy to improve their lower body extremities. It tracks a person's typical walking pattern and redirects it into a straighter line by keeping hips centered and posture upright.

The device assists with daily functions like crouching to grab something from a lower shelf. It locks around the knee and extends it further than what a person with CP typically can, without putting extreme weight on the knees.

After 2020

Oct 2021

Stasiam

An app that combines video games with physical therapy. For weak hip strength, a racing game lets you control a car by holding steering wheels and widening your hips and thighs to move left or right.

For foot-dragging issues, a Super Mario-style game requires stepping on a board to jump on Goombas. Squatting helps with tube entry or pole jumps, improving joint strength and posture. Works with Nintendo Ring Con, dance pads, and Wii balance boards.

Jan 2024

GoMove.org

An app that allows people with CP to do exercises at home, catered to individual needs and abilities. Users can set goals such as tying shoelaces, buttoning up a shirt, or going up stairs without a railing.

Therapists work with clients to help set goals and celebrate achievements.

Part B: Future Technology

Brain Computer Interface

It's already being developed in invasive and non-invasive types, but they only record signals and thoughts. I want a BCI that can send brain signals to parts of the body that aren't receiving the signals they need to function properly - which is the cause of Cerebral Palsy.

A device would go on the outside of the skull, not inside, so that the person's thoughts could stay private.

Smaller Exoskeleton

Has the same support the exoskeleton has except that it's smaller - kind of like how a computer can be on a big laptop or a small phone.

An electronic lock system in the shoe would assist with bending of one's knees without over-pressuring them. A spring could be implanted on the bottom of the shoe to assist with jumping.

Balance Tracker

This would be an app on your iPhone connected to your Apple Watch. The Balance Tracker will make your watch beep when it senses low balance or that you are going off balance.

At the end of the day, the app would give you an average balance score displayed on a chart like an EKG, showing spikes at times when balance was lost.
